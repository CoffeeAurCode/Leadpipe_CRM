Vous, Not Tu — Fixing the Agent's Own French

Sessions 8–12 taught Max to understand garbled Québec-French city names. This session fixes the mirror-image problem: Max's own French was breaking. On a real call he told a caller "Tu n'es pas bien compris" — wrong grammar and a sudden slide from the polite vous into the chummy tu, mid-call — and mangled a screening question into word salad. The whole session is a tightly-scoped, prompt-only fix: lock the agent to formal vous, hand it the correct French line instead of making it translate live, and push the change to five live assistants — all without touching the English path, the complaint agent, or a single line of matching logic. Along the way: how we turned a vague request into a written decision memo of ranked options, and why we verified the accented characters byte-by-byte before trusting them.

1 · The evidence: when the agent breaks its own language

Plain-English explanation. We usually hunt for bugs where the machine mis-hears the caller. But an AI agent can also fail in the other direction: it speaks badly. The model that generates Max's words can produce a sentence that is grammatically wrong, or that switches politeness level partway through a call, or that simply comes out as word salad. These aren't speech-to-text errors (the caller was understood fine) — they're generation errors, and they're invisible unless you actually read the agent's own lines in a transcript.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). A leasing agent is the first impression of a property-management business. A renter who hears the "assistant" fumble basic French grammar concludes the company is sloppy — or that they're talking to a broken bot — and hangs up. We already knew the French calls were rough; this session separates "Max couldn't find the city" (Session 12) from "Max said something wrong" (this session). Same calls, two different defect classes.

How it works (analogy). Imagine a translator at a business meeting who understands every question perfectly but answers in clumsy, overly-casual phrasing — and halfway through starts addressing the CEO like an old friend. The comprehension is fine; the delivery torpedoes the deal. We're fixing delivery.

The raw evidence. Here is the moment from the French transcript that triggered R3. Watch Max's own two lines — not the caller's:

Max: ...est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel sait que vous regardez pour déménager? # ^ word-salad: a literal, broken calque of English "looking to move" User: Uh, we # STT garble — not the point here Max: Tu n'es pas bien compris, est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel est au courant... # ^ TWO faults: wrong grammar AND a flip from "vous" to "tu" mid-call

Two distinct generation faults in one breath: (1) "Tu n'es pas bien compris" is grammatically wrong — it literally means "you are not well understood" with an informal you, when Max meant "I didn't catch that" ("Je n'ai pas bien compris"); and (2) Max had been using the formal vous all call, then suddenly switched to the intimate tu. Both make a native speaker wince.

🔑 Key Concept — read the agent's lines, not just the caller's

When you debug a voice agent, it's natural to focus on what the caller said and whether the system understood it. But half the transcript is the agent talking, and that half can hide its own bugs — bad grammar, wrong tone, leaked internal phrasing. Always read both speakers. The most damaging errors are often the ones the agent confidently says.

2 · The T–V distinction: tu vs vous

Plain-English explanation. English has exactly one word for "you." French has two, and choosing the wrong one is a social error:

  • tu — informal/intimate "you." Used with family, close friends, children, pets. Using it with a stranger can sound presumptuous or disrespectful. The act of using it is called tutoiement.
  • vous — formal/polite "you" (and also plural "you"). The default for strangers, customers, and any professional setting. Using it is called vouvoiement.

Linguists call this the T–V distinction (from Latin tu and vos) — a feature many languages share (Spanish tú/usted, German du/Sie). Crucially, vous isn't just a different word — it changes the verb conjugation too: "you are" is tu es but vous êtes; "did you catch" is tu as compris vs vous avez compris. So picking vous ripples through the whole sentence.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). A leasing agent talking to a prospective customer must use vous, every time, for the whole call — exactly like a hotel concierge or a bank teller would. Slipping into tu (as Max did) reads as either disrespect or a malfunction. And because the caller might use tu themselves (some people are casual), the agent must hold vous regardless — it does not "mirror" the caller's register.

How it works (analogy). Think of vous as the spoken equivalent of addressing someone as "Sir/Madam" and "Ms. Tremblay." tu is first-name-and-a-backslap. A customer-service rep doesn't start calling a customer "buddy" halfway through a call just because the customer was relaxed — they stay professional. vous is that professional register, baked into the grammar.

Jargon decoder.

  • register — the level of formality of language (casual ↔ formal). Choosing vous sets a formal register.
  • tutoiement / vouvoiement — the French nouns for "using tu" / "using vous."
  • conjugation — how a verb changes form to match its subject (tu es vs vous êtes). vous takes its own verb endings.
  • calque — a word-for-word translation of an idiom from another language that comes out wrong (e.g. translating "looking to move" literally as "regardez pour déménager").
⚠️ Warning — register is invisible to an English-only reviewer

If you don't speak French, both "Je n'ai pas bien compris" and "Tu n'es pas bien compris" look like "some French." You cannot eyeball-review register. This is why the fix has to be explicit in the prompt (a rule the model follows) and why a native-speaker test call is the real acceptance test — see Verification.

3 · Why a language model drifts register and breaks grammar

Plain-English explanation. A language model generates text by predicting the next most-likely word given everything so far. It has no fixed "I am being formal now" switch — formality is just another pattern it absorbed from training data, where tu and vous both appear constantly. So unless you pin the register, the model can drift: a casual word from the caller, a long pause, or just probabilistic chance can nudge it from vous toward tu. The same goes for grammar: under pressure to translate an English instruction into French on the spot, it can produce a literal, broken calque.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). Max's prompt already had a [Language Policy] that locks the language (English vs French) for the whole call. But locking the language is not the same as locking the register. The policy said "speak French" — it never said "speak formal French and never switch to tu." That gap is precisely where the model drifted. The fix closes the gap with an explicit rule.

How it works (analogy). Picture an improv actor told only "perform this scene in French." They'll stay in French — but their formality wanders with the moment. If instead you tell them "you are a hotel concierge; address every guest as vous, never tu, the entire scene," you've removed the freedom that caused the drift. A system prompt is exactly that kind of standing direction.

The gap, in the actual prompt. Here is the relevant slice of the existing language policy — note it constrains language but is silent on register:

[Language Policy]
Detect the caller's language from their very first words and lock for the entire call.
- English -> ENGLISH ONLY for all remaining turns
- French  -> FRENCH ONLY for all remaining turns
...
FORBIDDEN at all times after language is detected:
- Mixing languages in one sentence
- Switching language for any reason
# Notice: it locks LANGUAGE (en/fr) but says NOTHING about formality (tu/vous).
# That silence is the bug surface — the model is free to drift into "tu".
🔑 Key Concept — "speaks the language" ≠ "controls the register"

Constraining which language a model uses does not constrain how formally it uses it. Tone, politeness, and register are separate axes that need their own explicit instructions. Whenever correct output depends on a social convention the model wasn't told to hold, assume it will eventually drift — and pin it.

4 · Prompt engineering: hand the model the line, don't make it translate

Plain-English explanation. There are two ways to get a French sentence out of a bilingual model. (A) Give it an English instruction ("ask whether their landlord knows they're looking") and trust it to translate live. (B) Give it the exact French sentence to say. Option A is where calques and register slips creep in, because the model is doing creative translation under time pressure on every call. Option B removes the translation step entirely — you've already written the correct line, so there's nothing for the model to get wrong.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). The broken word-salad question ("vous regardez pour déménager") came straight from on-the-fly translation: the prompt only had the English version of the landlord-awareness question, so the model improvised the French each call. By adding a vetted French template right next to the English one, we turn a risky live translation into a safe copy-the-line.

How it works (analogy). It's the difference between handing a waiter a script for the daily specials versus asking them to invent the wording every table. The script guarantees the phrasing is right; improvisation guarantees variety and the occasional clunker. For anything customer-facing and repeatable, script it.

Annotated code — the Q2 question, before and after.

# BEFORE — only the English line exists; the model must translate it live each French call
--- Q2: Landlord awareness ---
"Does your current landlord know you're looking?"
This is conversational only. Note the answer. Always continue regardless of response.

# AFTER — a vetted French (vous) template sits beside the English; nothing to improvise
--- Q2: Landlord awareness ---
English: "Does your current landlord know you're looking?"
French (vous): "Est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel sait que vous cherchez à déménager?"
This is conversational only. Note the answer. Always continue regardless of response.

Note the French template uses vous ("vous cherchez") and the idiomatic "cherchez à déménager" ("looking to move") instead of the broken literal calque. The model now reads a correct line rather than generating one.

And the register rule itself — the new block we added to the prompt, which is what stops the tu drift everywhere, not just in Q2:

[French Register — STRICT]
On French calls, always address the caller with the formal vous. NEVER use tu, t', or tu-conjugated
verbs — stay in vous for the entire call, even if the caller uses tu.
Keep your own French grammatical and natural. If you did not catch something, say
"Je n'ai pas bien compris, pourriez-vous répéter?" — never "Tu n'es pas bien compris" or any tu form.
💡 Tip — name the failure in the rule

Notice the rule literally quotes the wrong line ("never 'Tu n'es pas bien compris'") and supplies the right one. Showing the model the exact mistake to avoid, plus the exact replacement, is far stronger than an abstract "be formal." When you've seen a concrete bad output, paste it into the prompt as a negative example.

5 · The decision memo: ranked options before action

Plain-English explanation. Your first request was open-ended: "find ways to refine the French." The wrong response is to grab the first idea and start coding. The right response is a decision memo — a short written document that lays out the realistic options, ranks them, and gives each one honest pros, cons, risk, and effort, so the person who owns the trade-off (you) can choose. The agent's job there is analysis, not action.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). "Refine the French" could mean anything from a one-line prompt tweak to swapping the speech-to-text engine. Those differ wildly in risk and effort. Writing them down as R1–R5 with trade-offs let you see that R3 (the register fix) was the safest, highest-value first step, and defer the riskier transcriber change (R2) behind a test gate. The memo also becomes a durable artifact: the four options you didn't pick are now recorded for later, not lost.

How it works (analogy). It's a contractor who, asked "improve my kitchen," comes back with a one-page quote: "Option A: repaint, $500, 1 day, low risk. Option B: new cabinets, $6k, 1 week. Option C: knock down a wall, $20k, permits required." You choose with eyes open. Charging in to knock down a wall because they assumed that's what "improve" meant would be reckless.

🔑 Key Concept — separate "analysis" from "action" turns

When a request is exploratory ("find ways…"), the deliverable is a recommendation, not a commit. Produce the options, mark a recommended path, and let the owner decide. It costs one extra round-trip and saves you from building the wrong thing — especially when the explicit constraint is "don't break anything."

6 · Non-breaking change design: additive & scoped

Plain-English explanation. Your hard constraint was "without degrading or breaking the existing agent." A change is non-breaking when it can only add behaviour in a tightly bounded place, never alter or remove behaviour that something else depends on. The way you guarantee that isn't hope — it's scoping: prove the edit touches only one code path and is invisible to every other path.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). The same file, vapi_agent_config.py, defines three agents: the lease agent (Max), the complaint agent (Alex), and the legacy tenant agent. They share helper functions. A careless edit to a shared piece could change all three. R3 is safe because every edit lands inside _LEASE_SYSTEM_PROMPT_BASE — a string used only by the lease agent — and only adds French-call instructions. The English path is untouched (the new rules start with "On French calls"), and the complaint agent never reads this variable at all.

How it works (analogy). It's adding a new road sign on one street versus re-timing every traffic light in the city. The sign affects only the cars on that street; nothing else in the grid can change behaviour because nothing else can even see it. Good scoping makes the "blast radius" of a change provably tiny.

vapi_agent_config.py ├── SYSTEM_PROMPT .............. legacy tenant agent (untouched) ├── COMPLAINT_SYSTEM_PROMPT .... complaint agent "Alex" (untouched) └── _LEASE_SYSTEM_PROMPT_BASE .. lease agent "Max" <-- R3 edits land ONLY here ├── [Language Policy] (existing) ├── [French Register — STRICT] <-- NEW block (French calls only) └── --- Q2: Landlord awareness --- <-- French template added English calls: the new rules say "On French calls..." -> no effect Complaint/tenant agents: never read this variable -> no effect
🔑 Key Concept — prove the blast radius, don't assert it

"This won't break anything" is a claim you should be able to demonstrate: name the exact symbol you edited, show what reads it, and confirm everything else is blind to it. Here the proof is one sentence — the edits are inside a variable only the lease agent uses, and they're gated on "French calls." Scope first, then you can promise safety honestly.

7 · One deploy surface or two? Prompt-only changes

Plain-English explanation. A voice agent has two places its behaviour can live: the backend code (our FastAPI server on Render, which the tools call) and the VAPI assistant config (the prompt, voice, and tool definitions, stored at VAPI). A change isn't live until every surface it touches is deployed. The trick is recognising which surfaces a given change touches. Session 12's change touched both (it edited leasing.py and the prompt) — so it needed two deploys. R3 edits only the prompt, so it needs only the config push. One surface.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). Knowing this prevents two opposite mistakes: under-deploying (Session 12's cliff-hanger — the config went live but the backend half didn't, so neither fix fully worked) and over-deploying (needlessly redeploying the Render backend for a pure-prompt change, risking downtime for no reason). For R3, the correct and complete deploy is a single command: update_lease_agents.py, which pushes the new prompt to every live assistant.

How it works (analogy). If you change the menu (the prompt), you reprint the menu. You don't also re-wire the kitchen (the backend) — the kitchen didn't change. Matching the deploy to what actually changed keeps you fast and safe.

Jargon decoder.

  • assistant config — the JSON bundle at VAPI (prompt + voice + tools) that defines an agent. Changed by re-pushing via the update script.
  • per-manager assistant — each property manager gets their own VAPI lease assistant; the script loops over all of them (here, 4) plus 1 shared agent.
  • dry-run — running the deploy in preview mode (--dry-run) that reports what would change without changing it. Always do this first.
  • idempotent — re-running the same push produces the same result; safe to run twice.
💡 Tip — ask "what files did I touch?" to find your deploy surfaces

If your diff is only in the prompt strings of vapi_agent_config.py → config push only. If it's also in a route like leasing.py or in requirements.txt → you also need a backend deploy. The files you edited are the checklist of surfaces to ship.

8 · UTF-8 on Windows: verify the bytes, don't trust the glyph

Plain-English explanation. Accented characters like é, à, and the em-dash — aren't single bytes — they're encoded, almost always in UTF-8 (the universal text encoding). If a tool writes the file in the wrong encoding, an é can silently turn into garbage like Ã© — a phenomenon called mojibake. On Windows especially (whose default code page isn't UTF-8), this is a real hazard when a program writes French text. So after writing accented content, you verify it came out as clean UTF-8 instead of assuming.

Why we need it (for CRM specifically). R3 is made of accented French — "propriétaire," "répéter," "déménager." If those got mojibake'd into the prompt, the agent could read corrupted instructions, or the file could fail to parse, or the agent could even speak the garbage. A previous session was burned by a false mojibake scare (a console display artifact that looked like corruption but wasn't), so the discipline is: check the actual file bytes, not what a terminal happens to render.

How it works (analogy). It's spell-checking a letter you dictated to someone else before mailing it. You don't assume they transcribed the accents correctly — you read it back. Here we "read it back" by grepping the file for the exact French words and confirming they appear intact.

How we verified. A search for the French strings, plus a Python parse, both confirmed clean output:

$ # grep the file for the accented words we just wrote 1035:[French Register — STRICT] 1039:"Je n'ai pas bien compris, pourriez-vous répéter?" — never "Tu n'es pas bien compris"... 1190:French (vous): "Est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel sait que vous cherchez à déménager?" # ^ é, à, — all render correctly -> clean UTF-8, not "Ã©" / "Ã " mojibake $ python -c "import ast; ast.parse(open('vapi_agent_config.py',encoding='utf-8').read()); print('syntax OK')" syntax OK

Jargon decoder.

  • UTF-8 — the dominant text encoding; represents é as two bytes, the em-dash as three. Read with the right encoding, it shows the right glyph.
  • mojibake — garbled text from decoding bytes with the wrong encoding (Japanese for "character transformation"); the classic tell is Ã© where you expected é.
  • code page — Windows' legacy notion of "which encoding"; its non-UTF-8 default is why Windows is a mojibake hotspot.
⚠️ Warning — a garbled terminal isn't proof of a garbled file

Console windows often can't display UTF-8 and show � or Ã© even when the file on disk is perfect (we saw exactly this in the deploy output's em-dashes). Verify the file — grep it, or read it with an explicit encoding="utf-8" — before declaring corruption. Don't fix a bug that only exists in your terminal's font.

🐛 Bug 1 — "Tu n'es pas bien compris": wrong grammar + a register flip

🚨 The Symptom

On a French call, after the caller's words came back garbled, Max replied: "Tu n'es pas bien compris, est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel est au courant…" Two things are wrong: the phrase literally means "you are not well understood" (informal you) when Max meant "I didn't catch that," and it uses tu after Max had spoken vous the entire call.

🔍 The Diagnosis

We read Max's own lines across the transcript and confirmed he'd been on vous until this turn — so this was a mid-call drift, not a consistent style. Then we read the system prompt's [Language Policy] and found it locks the language (English vs French) but says nothing about register (formal vs informal). The instruction simply wasn't there for the model to follow.

🧠 The Root Cause

A language model drifts on any axis you don't pin (Concept 3). "Speak French" was constrained; "speak formal French, always vous" was not. Under the nudge of a garbled turn, the model's next-word prediction wandered into a tu construction — and botched the grammar of the recovery phrase while doing so. It's a missing-instruction bug, not a model defect: the prompt under-specified the social register.

🔧 The Fix

Add the [French Register — STRICT] block (Step 3): mandate vous for the whole call, ban tu/t'/tu-conjugations even if the caller uses tu, and quote the correct recovery line while naming the wrong one as forbidden.

# BEFORE — no register rule anywhere; the model is free to say (and did say):
"Tu n'es pas bien compris"        # informal "tu" + wrong subject (means "you", not "I")

# AFTER — the prompt pins the register and supplies the correct line verbatim:
"Je n'ai pas bien compris, pourriez-vous répéter?"   # formal vous, correct grammar
# ...plus the standing rule: "always vous, NEVER tu, for the entire call."
🛡️ The Lesson

If correct output depends on a convention (politeness, tone, format) the model wasn't explicitly told to hold, it will eventually break it. Pin every axis that matters. And when you've captured a concrete bad line, put it in the prompt as a named negative example — "never say X, say Y" beats "be formal."

🐛 Bug 2 — The word-salad screening question (a live-translation calque)

🚨 The Symptom

Max's landlord-awareness question came out as "…est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel sait que vous regardez pour déménager?" — a clumsy, broken French sentence. "regardez pour déménager" is a literal word-for-word rendering of English "looking to move" that no native speaker would say.

🔍 The Diagnosis

We located the Q2 step in the prompt and saw it contained only the English version of the question. So on every French call the model had to translate it live — and live translation of an idiom is exactly where a calque (Concept 2) sneaks in. The bug wasn't random; it was structural: we were asking the model to improvise a translation each time.

🧠 The Root Cause

On-the-fly translation under time pressure (Concept 4). Given only an English line, the model produced a literal calque instead of the idiomatic French. The prompt offered no canonical French phrasing to copy, so it generated one — and got the idiom wrong.

🔧 The Fix

Add a vetted French (vous) template beside the English (Step 4), using the idiomatic "cherchez à déménager." Now there's nothing to translate — the model reads a correct line.

# BEFORE — only English; model improvises the French each call -> calque
"Does your current landlord know you're looking?"
# model emitted: "...vous regardez pour déménager?"   (broken)

# AFTER — supply the correct French line; nothing to improvise
French (vous): "Est-ce que votre propriétaire actuel sait que vous cherchez à déménager?"
🛡️ The Lesson

Don't make a model translate customer-facing idioms live if you can pre-write them. For any repeatable line that must be correct, script the exact wording in the target language. Save improvisation for genuinely dynamic content.

🧯 Troubleshooting / Common Errors

The agent still says "tu" on a French call after the change

Cause & fix: most likely the config push didn't run or didn't reach that manager's assistant — re-run update_lease_agents.py and confirm 5 updated, 0 failed. If it did run and tu persists, reinforce the rule (move the [French Register] block earlier, or add a one-line reminder in the qualification section); models weight repeated/earlier instructions more heavily.

French text shows as Ã© / â€" in an editor or the terminal

Cause: something decoded UTF-8 bytes with the wrong encoding (Windows code-page default). Fix: first determine whether it's the file or just the display — read the file with open(path, encoding="utf-8"). If the bytes are fine, it's only your terminal's font/codepage (harmless). If the file itself is corrupted, rewrite it as UTF-8.

SyntaxError after editing the prompt string

Cause: the prompt lives in a triple-quoted Python string; an accidental stray """ or an unbalanced quote inside it can terminate the string early. Fix: run the ast.parse check from Verification step 2; it points to the line. Keep edits inside the existing triple-quoted block.

update_lease_agents.py says "0 per-manager"

Cause: no rows in manager_vapi_config have vapi_provisioning_status='active'. Fix: confirm at least one manager has been provisioned (Session 12's context), and that .env points at the right Supabase project.

The session HTML code blocks render garbled

Cause: a raw <, >, or & inside a <pre><code> block confuses the in-page tokenizer. Fix: HTML-escape them (&lt;, &gt;, &amp;) — e.g. write -&gt; for ->.
